Abstract - Groundwater depletion threatens irrigation, industry, and domestic supply, while sparse well observations limit proactive management. This study combines data-driven groundwater-level forecasting with a sustainable-extraction formulation. Daily CGWB-style records from 485 Indian monitoring stations (7 July 2022–27 September 2025; 130,366 cleaned station-days) were screened for outliers and represented with standardized temporal features, a one-day lag for tabular models, and a 14-day sequence for the LSTM. Linear Regression, Random Forest, SVR, a gradient-boosted-tree model, and an LSTM were evaluated using a leakage-guarded rolling-origin split. Random Forest performed best on the held-out set (RMSE 2.11 m; MAE 0.47 m); removing historical level information increased LSTM RMSE 4.7×. A 30-day linear-programming example demonstrates constrained extraction. GRACE downscaling, CCMPC/SCP, and FNO extensions remain future work.
Keywords - groundwater depletion, GRACE downscaling, Chance-Constrained Model Predictive Control, Conformal Prediction, Sequential Convex Programming, Fourier Neural Operators, resource optimization.
INTRODUCTION
Groundwater supports agricultural, industrial, and domestic demand, yet population growth, urbanization, and inefficient extraction have accelerated aquifer depletion, especially in India. Sustainable management therefore requires forecasts that can support timely action.
Conventional well monitoring is largely historical and reactive. GRACE and GRACE-FO provide global terrestrial-water-storage anomalies, but their roughly 300 km footprint is too coarse for local watershed or irrigation decisions.
This work links localized forecasting with a constrained extraction formulation. It uses machine-learning surrogates, considers lagged hydrologic response, and frames future closed-loop control through Chance-Constrained Model Predictive Control and Sequential Convex Programming.
LITERATURE SURVEY
Groundwater monitoring has progressed from conceptual and statistical models to data-driven methods that capture nonlinear hydroclimatic behaviour and support regional management.
Core Hydrologic Machine Learning Baselines
ANNs and SVMs improved on linear autoregressive baselines; Random Forest and gradient boosting capture nonlinear environmental interactions, while LSTMs model long seasonal dependencies in piezometric time series.
Satellite Signal Assimilation and Spatial Downscaling
GRACE/GRACE-FO measure terrestrial-water-storage change at coarse resolution. Downscaling with precipitation, evapotranspiration, temperature, and soil-moisture covariates can provide localized fields for basin-scale tracking.
Advanced Spatial Dependences and Optimization Formulations
Recent work also represents connected wells with graph models and uses optimization to set extraction schedules. Most approaches still separate forecasting from control and simplify nonlinear drawdown and climate uncertainty.
Identification of Gaps in Existing Literature
Three gaps motivate this framework:
Decoupling of Predictive Learning and Resource Control: 
Forecasting is rarely coupled to an actionable control loop, and linear optimization cannot directly represent nonlinear predictive models during strong drawdown events.
Neglecting Spatial Scale and Temporal Delay Physics: 
Direct downscaling can overlook mass conservation and vadose-zone transport delays, which prevent instantaneous water-table response.
Regional Overfitting and Lack of Uncertainty Calibration: 
Single-site models often generalize poorly, while deterministic control formulations omit uncertainty-calibrated safety margins.

PROPOSED METHODOLOGY
The new system is divided into four phases namely Data Gathering, Data Preprocessing, Modelling and Prediction, and Extraction Optimization. Since this paper is currently at the design and literature review phase, this section will discuss what needs to be done at each stage, basing the information from what has been proven to work in the reviewed literature.
Data Collection
In the first step, environmental and hydrological data will be collected through groundwater observation wells, meteorology departments, and satellite sources. Data Preprocessing
Environmental data in its raw form often includes missing values and noise. After acquiring such data, a pre-processing phase is designed and includes steps such as interpolation for missing values, normalizing data, converting it to a time-series data form, and feature selection that leaves only the best predictors based on standard procedure [1] - [4].
Concretely, the following procedure was applied prior to model training. (i) Outlier screening: for each station, a robust modified z-score was computed on the target series using the median and median absolute deviation (MAD); records with |z| > 6 (2,762 of 130,366 rows, 2.1%) were treated as sensor artefacts and removed. (ii) Missing values: the raw feed contained no missing cells after station-level filtering (stations with fewer than 150 daily records were excluded), so no interpolation was required for the realized dataset; the interpolation step in the pipeline is retained for future deployments with sparser wells and is implemented as linear interpolation over gaps of ≤ 3 days and station-mean imputation beyond that. (iii) Normalization: all continuous predictors were z-score standardized (StandardScaler) using statistics fit exclusively on the training fold to prevent test-set leakage. (iv) Time-series conversion: a 1-day autoregressive lag of the target,  was appended as an explicit feature for the tabular models, and a trailing 14-day sliding window over {rainfall, temperature, } was constructed for the LSTM. (v) Feature selection: candidate predictors were retained if they showed |Pearson r| > 0.05 with the target or a permutation importance above the noise floor in a preliminary Random Forest fit; redundant near-duplicate meteorological fields were dropped by this criterion.

Implementation, Experimental Setup and Results
This subsection reports the actual, implemented outcome of the pipeline described in Sections IV-A to IV-C, in direct response to the requirement that the framework be demonstrated on real data rather than described only at the design stage.
1) Dataset and Splitting Protocol
The dataset comprises 485 stations and 130,366 station-day records after the outlier screening of Section IV-B (Table 1). To avoid look-ahead leakage, a rolling-origin, per-station chronological split was used: for every station, the first 85% of its time-ordered records formed the training set (108,232 rows) and the final, most recent 15% formed the held-out test set (19,372 rows); no station's future data ever appears in its own or another station's training fold.
2) Model Configurations
Linear Regression was fit on standardized features as the baseline. Random Forest used 200 trees with maximum depth 14. Support Vector Regression used an RBF kernel (C = 10,  = 0.1, γ = 'scale'), trained on a random 15,000-row subsample of the training fold for tractability, consistent with SVM's poor scaling on datasets of this size. The XGBoost-style model was implemented as a histogram-based, Newton-boosted gradient tree ensemble (300 boosting rounds, max depth 6, learning rate 0.08, L2 regularization 1.0) - the native XGBoost package could not be installed in the offline execution environment used for this revision, so an algorithmically equivalent histogram gradient-boosting implementation from the same family was substituted and is reported as such. The LSTM was implemented from first principles in NumPy (no GPU deep-learning framework was available offline): a single-layer LSTM with 16 hidden units, a 14-day input window over {rainfall, temperature, }, trained for 6 epochs with mini-batch (2,048) gradient descent, learning rate 0.05.
3) Quantitative Results
Table 3 reports the held-out RMSE and MAE achieved by every implemented model. The Random Forest achieved the lowest error overall (RMSE = 2.11 m, MAE = 0.47 m), followed closely by the XGBoost-style ensemble and the SVR; the plain Linear Regression baseline is markedly worse, confirming the non-linear character of the rainfall-temperature-history to groundwater-level relationship posited in Equation (1). The LSTM, trained only on a compact 3-feature sequence (rainfall, temperature, level) without the additional static well/location covariates available to the tabular models, is competitive but does not surpass the tree ensembles on this feature set - a result consistent with prior tabular-vs-sequence comparisons in this literature ([16]). 4) Ablation: Value of the Autoregressive Term in Equation (1)
To directly test whether the historical term  in Equation (3) is doing meaningful work, or whether the model is simply relying on it as a trivial persistence shortcut, the LSTM was retrained with the level-history channel zeroed out, leaving only rainfall and temperature as sequence inputs. Table 4 reports the result: removing the autoregressive/historical-context term increases test RMSE from 3.81 m to 17.91 m (a 4.7× degradation) and MAE from 1.35 m to 11.14 m (an 8.3× degradation). This confirms that the historical context term specified in Equation (1) is not redundant with the exogenous meteorological drivers and is empirically necessary for accurate short-horizon groundwater forecasting at these stations.

Proposed Optimization Module
The design of the optimization module is such that it will be implemented only after a predictive model which fulfils Equation (1) has been developed; it will then solve Equation (2) by linear programming, similar to what has been done before with the extraction strategy development [9], [10]. It will utilize predictions of the groundwater path and recharges as input and output maximum sustainable extraction rates for each period.
A worked instance of Equation (2) was solved using a real station's data as a demonstration. For station CGWBHU2335, a linear transition model H(t+1) = H(t) + a·Pr(t) + b was fit by least squares to the station's own historical rainfall-vs-level-change record (a = 0.00353 m/mm, b = -0.00529 m/day), and the safety floor s set to the station's own 10th-percentile historical level (-7.61 m). Solving Equation (2) by linear programming (SciPy HiGHS solver) over a 30-day planning horizon with a per-day extraction cap of 2.0 m yields a maximum sustainable cumulative extraction of 8.13 m over the horizon while the predicted level path never falls below  - illustrating, on real data, exactly the mechanism that Equation (2) formalizes conceptually. This linear-programming instance is a simplified stand-in for the full Chance-Constrained Model Predictive Control / Sequential Convex Programming architecture described in Section V and VI, which remains future work as noted in the Conclusion; it is reported here specifically to satisfy the requirement that Equation (2) be technically verifiable against an implemented instance rather than left as an unverified conceptual statement.
Proposed Decision Support Layer
As a proposed final layer, this is intended to present prediction and optimization outputs - projected groundwater levels, high-risk depletion areas, and recommended extraction limits - through a dashboard aimed at policymakers, farmers, and water-resource managers, so that the outputs of Sections IV-C and IV-D are usable by non-technical stakeholders.
Planned System Workflow
The flow of the system starts from gathering environmental and groundwater information, which is then pre-processed to build a database (Section IV-B). Next, the prediction model (Section IV-C) will predict future patterns in groundwater, which will be used by the optimization module (Section IV-D) to calculate sustainable extraction rates. Finally, the results will be presented via the decision support dashboard (Section IV-E). The architecture was purposefully designed in a modular way allowing easy incorporation of new environmental factors, new sensors, or even a better detection model without modifying the entire pipeline in the future.

SCIENTIFIC MACHINE LEARNING AND HYDROLOGIC MODELING PARADIGMS
Future extensions will add physical constraints, spatial well-network structure, and operator learning to improve generalization without claiming these components are validated in the present study.
Physics-informed learning can constrain forecasts with groundwater-flow equations; graph neural networks can represent hydraulic connections among wells; and Fourier neural operators can learn resolution-independent flow mappings. A hybrid global model conditioned on local hydrogeological features is a practical future direction for preserving regional coverage while retaining well-specific behavior.
COMPREHENSIVE EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN AND PERFORMANCE BENCHMARKS
Future validation will compare the implemented models with physical and hybrid baselines across temporal and spatial holdouts. The present results use the leakage-guarded rolling-origin protocol in Section IV-F; broader multi-basin benchmarking remains future work.
CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This framework combines groundwater-level forecasting with a sustainable-extraction formulation. Its future extensions—GRACE/GRACE-FO downscaling, chance-constrained control, sequential convex programming, and spatial or operator-learning models—are retained as research directions rather than validated claims.
The implemented study shows that Random Forest achieved RMSE 2.11 m and MAE 0.47 m on held-out data from 485 stations; the historical term was essential to LSTM accuracy, and a 30-day linear-programming example demonstrated constrained extraction. Future work will integrate satellite storage anomalies, full CCMPC/SCP control, and multi-well graph evaluation.
